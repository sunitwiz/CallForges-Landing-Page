How to set up an IVR phone menu

Your office manager spends Monday mornings passing calls along. The caller wanted billing, she does scheduling, and billing is on another line. A phone menu sorts those calls before anyone picks up. To set one up, pick three or four options, write the message with each reason before its key, then build it. In CallForges that means an IVR menu step in your number's call workflow, a step under each key, and publishing it.

CallForges team · 27 November 2026

Key takeaways
  • Keep the first menu to three or four options, with the most common reason on key 1.
  • Say the reason before the key ("For billing, press 2"), and put your welcome in the menu message itself.
  • Give callers a way to reach a person, and send anyone who presses nothing somewhere useful.

How many options should a phone menu have?

Three or four on the first menu. Callers hear the options once, in order, and hold them in their head while they decide, so each extra option makes the first ones harder to keep. What is an IVR? covers whether you need one at all.

If your callers' reasons won't fit into four, group them by the person or team who answers. "Service" can cover repairs and warranty questions if one team takes both. Put the most common reason on key 1, so most callers press it and never hear the rest; last month's call history shows which one that is.

A short menu means broad options, and a few callers won't be sure which one is theirs. Give them a key that rings a person.

What should an IVR menu say?

Your business name, then the options, then how to reach a person. Callers listen for their own reason, so give the reason first and the key second. "For billing, press 2" lets a caller stop listening the moment they hear "billing". "Press 2 for billing" makes them hold on to a number until they find out whether it's theirs.

Cut anything that delays the first option. "Please listen carefully, as our menu options have changed" costs every caller several seconds, and your regulars stopped hearing it long ago. Skip the website plug too: anyone calling has already picked the phone. Two scripts to start from:

Office hours menu

"Thanks for calling [Business]. For a repair or a service visit, press 1. For billing, press 2. To talk to someone at the front desk, press 0."

After-hours menu

"Thanks for calling [Business]. We're closed right now and open again at 8am. If you have a burst pipe, a leak or no heat, press 1 to reach the person on call. For anything else, press 2 to leave a message, and we'll call you back in the morning."

An emergency key and a way to a person

After hours, the emergency key is the reason to have a menu at all. Write down what counts before you record anything, such as a burst pipe or no heat in January, and say it in the menu. Send that key to the on-call person's mobile. Callers sort themselves, and the dripping tap waits for morning. If a caller could be in danger, a gas smell for example, tell them to hang up and call 911 (or your local emergency number) before you offer any key.

In office hours, give every menu a way to a person; some callers won't find their reason on the list. One Hacker News commenter described a menu of X, Y and Z options: they pressed 0 to reach a person and heard "I'm sorry. I don't recognize that menu option. To hear the options again, please press 9" (Hacker News, 2025). Make 0 ring the front desk, and say so at the end of the menu.

Then decide where callers go when they press nothing. Some are driving, and some missed the options. We'd send them to the front desk in office hours and to voicemail at night, never back to the start on a loop.

  2. Click the IVR menu step to open its panel. Under Keys, give each key a short label, such as Key 1 "Emergency". Click Add key for each extra option.
  3. On the canvas, add a step under each key: Ring users or teams for a team, Forward call for a mobile (with its country code), or Voicemail.
  4. Turn on Repeat the menu so callers can hear the options again.
  5. Write the Menu message (optional). Choose Text to speech and type it, Custom message to upload an audio file, or Record audio to record it yourself. Name the keys in your message, or leave them out and CallForges reads the options after it. Put your welcome in this message too: keys a caller presses during an Audio message before the menu are lost.
  6. Pick the Voice and Language callers hear.
  7. Put a step under No valid key, the path for callers who don't press a key on the menu. We'd ring the front desk by day and take a Voicemail at night.
  8. Click Save draft, then Publish. If something needs fixing, Can't publish yet lists each problem with its step.
  9. Click Numbers, pick your numbers and click Save numbers.

Test it before your customers do

Once it's published, call your number from your mobile and press every key. Call again and press nothing, then once more with a key that isn't on the menu. Each key should ring the phone you meant, and the caller who pressed nothing should reach a person or a voicemail. A path with nothing under it ends the call. If the menu only plays at night, call after closing time; a daytime test only proves your business hours path.

Listen to the voice as a stranger would. A typed message that read well on screen can rush the options together when it's read out, so end each option with a period. And go back to the menu whenever your hours or your team change. A menu that still says "ask for Dave" six months after Dave left sends callers nowhere.
