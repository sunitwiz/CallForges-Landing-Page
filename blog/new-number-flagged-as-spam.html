Why is my new business number flagged as spam?

You bought a fresh number on Monday so your new rep wouldn't burn the old one. By Wednesday, after 150 dials, a prospect tells you it came up "Spam Likely". A new number gets labeled for one of two reasons: it carries a previous owner's history, or it had no history at all and then started calling hundreds of people a day. Slow it down, register it, and check what the networks see.

CallForges team · 22 November 2026

Key takeaways
  • The label comes from the network of the person you call, or an app on their phone, and it's based on how the number calls.
  • A "new" number may be recycled, and a quiet number that suddenly makes a burst of calls looks like a robocaller.
  • Buying yet another number rarely helps. Ramp up slowly, register the business and the number, then dispute any label that stays.

Why a brand-new number shows up as spam

The warning comes from the network of the person you call, or a call-blocking app on their phone. It scores every number that calls in, and a low score puts a warning on the screen. T-Mobile shows "Scam Likely" and AT&T shows "Spam Risk" (FCC, 2021). These labels are carrier policy, not law, so each network scores its own way and changes it without notice.

The FCC describes these warnings as "generally based on analytics" (FCC, 2023). That's why being new doesn't protect you. The analytics see a number with no track record doing something that looks like a robocaller's opening week.

Real estate investors on BiggerPockets ask how to stop cold calls saying "scam" and why their number shows up as spam, often days after switching numbers.

The three causes, most likely first

Check them in this order, because the first two can catch a number you've barely used.

It isn't really new

Phone numbers get recycled. Carriers disconnect about 35 million US numbers a year and make them available to new owners (FCC, 2018). Labels are tied to the number, so yours can arrive with someone else's rating, including a robocaller's.

It went from silent to busy overnight

Volume alone can trip the scoring. Credit unions told the FCC that providers said analytics marked their calls as potential spam "based solely on the quantity of outbound calls" (FCC, 2021). A number that made zero calls last week and 200 today fits that pattern.

The calls themselves look like robocalls

Among the signs of illegal calling the FCC encourages phone companies to watch are "call duration", "call completion ratios" and "large bursts of calls in a short time frame" (FCC, 2023). A cold list where nine in ten calls go unanswered, plus a rep who hangs up after two rings, hands the analytics all three. People who never asked to hear from you also tap "report".

Should you just buy another number?

We wouldn't. It's the first thing someone on the floor suggests, and it feels like it works for a few days. Then the replacement gets the same label, because the scoring follows how you call. Now you're paying for a stack of numbers, and leads who call back reach a line nobody answers.

Fix the pattern first, then decide how many numbers you need. More numbers can make sense when each rep or team gets its own, so each one carries a smaller share of the day's calls. Rotating numbers to hide from the scoring is a different thing, and we'd skip it. Our post on why rotating numbers backfires goes deeper.

How to warm up a new number

Treat a new number like a new rep: give it a lighter first week. This is a best practice, not a carrier rule, and no network publishes a safe number of calls. As an example of the shape we'd use:

  1. Before the first dial, register the business and the number, and set up the caller ID name (both below).
  2. In week one, stay well below your normal daily volume and call leads who know you first, like callbacks and inbound leads. They answer, and answered calls of a normal length look nothing like a robocall.
  3. In weeks two and three, raise the daily dials step by step while the pickup rate holds.
  4. From then on, let calls ring out, keep a pause between calls, and call people in their own working hours.

The trade-off is speed. You'll get through fewer leads in the first fortnight, and we think that beats burning a number in three days and starting over.

How to check and dispute a label

Call a phone on each big network from the number and look at the screen. If a label shows, dispute it with the network that shows it. The industry's own guidance says to review your calling habits first, and that standard requests should be answered within 2 business days, as a best practice rather than a guarantee (USTelecom, 2026). The forms for each network, and what to write in them, are in Phone number reputation: why calls get labeled spam.

The Free Caller Registry is one free form that shares your numbers with the three call-analytics companies behind the big US networks (Free Caller Registry, checked 2026). It doesn't promise a fix, but it tells them who you are before your first busy day.
