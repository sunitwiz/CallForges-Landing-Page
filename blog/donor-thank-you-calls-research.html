Donor thank-you calls: what the research says

It's January, your year-end appeal brought in 400 first-time donors, and your development director has a board call list ready. Should you make those thank-you calls? The biggest study we know of found that thank-you calls made by paid callers to new donors didn't change whether they gave again. Keep calling if you like, but make each call worth the donor's time, and test whether your own calls work.

CallForges team · 26 November 2026

Key takeaways
  • In a large randomized study, thank-you calls from paid callers had no effect on whether new donors gave again.
  • Fundraisers expected a big lift, so the habit may be running on belief more than evidence.
  • Calls from board members or volunteers weren't tested. Hold back a random group and measure your own results.

What the study found

Researchers ran experiments with nearly 70 public television stations and a national nonprofit over six years, covering nearly 600,000 new donors and 500,000 calls (USC Schaeffer Center, 2019). New donors were randomly split: some got a thank-you call three to seven months after their first gift, and some didn't. Paid callers made the calls.

The calls "had no impact on donor behavior", in the researchers' words (same source). About 30% of new donors gave again the next year, with or without a call. The authors put the cost at around $1 per call, with no extra revenue to show for it. They did see some evidence that donors who reacted well to the call were more likely to give again.

The surprise was the forecasts. Before the results, about 200 fundraising professionals predicted retention of about 50% for donors who got a call, well above what happened. Experience didn't make their guesses better (same source).

What it means for your thank-you calls

The study tested one kind of call: a paid caller, months after the gift, at large organizations. It didn't test calls from board members or volunteers, which the authors call an open question. If your board members make the calls, nobody has measured whether that works.

We'd take three lessons from it:

  • Give the donor something new in the call, like what their gift paid for, so it's worth answering.
  • Watch the cost. Staff time spent dialing 400 numbers is time not spent on major donors or grant reports.
  • Measure it yourself. Your donors, your callers and your timing differ from the study's.

What to say on the call

Keep it short and about them. We'd open with their name and the gift ("Hi Maria, I'm calling from the food bank to thank you for your gift in December"), then give one concrete thing it did, like the number of meals or the program it joined. Ask one question you actually want answered, such as what made them give. Then let them go. No second ask: a thank-you that turns into a request for money teaches donors to stop picking up.

If you reach voicemail, leave the same thank-you in under 30 seconds and don't ask them to call back. They owe you nothing.

How to test thank-you calls at your nonprofit

You can run a small version of the study with a spreadsheet. The trick is letting chance decide who gets a call, so the two groups only differ by the call.

  1. Take this season's new donors and put them in random order.
  2. Call the first half. Leave the second half uncalled.
  3. Log every call's outcome: reached, voicemail, or no answer.
  4. A year on, compare how many in each half gave again, and how much.

With a few hundred donors the gap needs to be large before you can trust it, so don't read much into a few points either way. If the called half clearly gives more, keep calling. If it doesn't, try changing one thing, such as calling within a week of the gift or having a board member call.

Which calling rules apply to nonprofits?

A thank-you call doesn't ask for money, so most solicitation rules don't touch it. The rules below are US federal law and depend on who makes the call.

RuleWho it applies toWhat it says (November 2026)
FCC: "telephone solicitation" (47 CFR 64.1200(f)(15))Calls by or on behalf of a tax-exempt nonprofitThey aren't telephone solicitations, so the national Do Not Call rules for solicitations don't apply to them.
FCC: prerecorded calls to home lines (47 CFR 64.1200(a)(3)(iv))A tax-exempt nonprofit's artificial or prerecorded voice calls to a residential lineAllowed without consent for no more than three calls in any 30 days, with an opt-out honored.
FTC: Telemarketing Sales Rule (16 CFR 310.4, 310.6)Telemarketers soliciting charitable contributionsA person who asked not to be called on behalf of that charity must not be called. The national registry part doesn't cover charitable solicitation calls.

A paid fundraising firm calling for you is a telemarketer under the FTC rule, so keep a list of donors who asked not to be called and honor it whoever dials. State charity laws add their own rules, so check the states you call.

This is general information, not legal advice.
