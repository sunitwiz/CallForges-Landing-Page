Phone number reputation: why calls get labeled spam

Your new rep's number picks up fine on Monday. By Thursday, after a few hundred dials, a prospect mentions it came up "Scam Likely". Phone number reputation is the score the called person's network, or an app on their phone, gives your number, and a low score puts a spam warning on your calls. Bursts of calls, short unanswered calls and spam reports pull it down, and a slower pace and registered numbers hold it up.

CallForges team · 18 November 2026

Key takeaways
  • The network of the person you call, or an app on their phone, sets the label, in its own words.
  • Volume alone can trip it, and so can short unanswered calls and spam reports.
  • Registering makes a label less likely but promises nothing. You dispute a wrong label with the network that shows it.

Who labels your calls, and what they show

AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon each work with an analytics company on their labels (USTelecom, checked 2026), and the FCC describes these warnings as "generally based on analytics" (FCC Seventh Report and Order, 2023). Labels are a carrier policy, so they vary by carrier and change without notice.

Each network words it differently:

Where the person's phone gets itWhat their screen shows
T-MobileScam Likely (FCC, 2021)
AT&TSpam Risk (FCC, 2021)
VerizonPotential Spam, Robo Caller or Potential Fraud (Verizon Call Filter FAQs, checked 2026)
The Phone app on many Android phonesA "suspected spam caller" warning (FCC, 2021)

A label can cost you the call itself. T-Mobile's Scam Block can block calls marked Scam Likely at the network, and Verizon's Call Filter can send spam calls straight to voicemail (FCC, 2021).

What lowers a number's reputation?

Analytics companies judge a number by its patterns, so a busy, honest sales number can look like a robocaller. Credit unions told the FCC that providers said analytics marked their calls as potential spam "based solely on the quantity of outbound calls" (FCC Second Report on Call Blocking, 2021). Four things pull a rating down:

  • Many calls in a burst. Verizon defines Potential Spam as "unwanted callers that may be calling indiscriminately or inappropriately to a large number of recipients" (Verizon Call Filter FAQs, checked 2026).
  • Short or unanswered calls. Among the signs of illegal calls the FCC encourages phone companies to watch are "call duration", "call completion ratios" and "large bursts of calls in a short time frame" (FCC, 2023).
  • Spam reports. Verizon Call Filter users can report numbers as spam, and the Phone app on Android lets users report suspicious calls (FCC, 2021).
  • A previous owner's past. Labels are tied to the number (FCC, 2021), and about 35 million US numbers are disconnected and made available for reassignment each year (FCC Second Report and Order, 2018). A number can reach you with someone else's rating.

Say three reps share one number and each makes 120 dials a day. That's 360 calls from one number, and if one in ten picks up, 324 end unanswered every day. Signed calls don't save it: Verizon won't "block or label a call made to a Verizon customer based solely on caller ID authentication information" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026).

Register your numbers with the analytics companies

The Free Caller Registry is one free form, run by the three call-analytics companies behind AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon, that shares your numbers with all of them (USTelecom, checked 2026). It's voluntary, for the business that makes the calls. It asks for your business name, address and website, a contact with a verified email, and why and how often you call, then takes up to 20 numbers typed in, or a file (checked 2026).

The registry says each company "may" use the data to improve its call management, that registering "does not guarantee redress", and that the data isn't used to show your caller ID name (same source). We'd still register a number before its first busy day: it's free, and the analytics then know your business and why you call.

How do you check or dispute a spam label?

Call a phone on each big network from the number and look at the screen. Dispute a label with the network that shows it. USTelecom, the phone companies' trade group, lists their forms (USTelecom, checked 2026):

NetworkWhere a business disputes a label
AT&Tatt.com/reviewmycalllabel
T-Mobilecallreporting.t-mobile.com
Verizonvoicespamfeedback.com

The industry's own guidance, a best practice rather than a law, says to look at your calling habits first (USTelecom Best Practices Relating to Redress Requests, 2026). A standard request should be answered within 2 business days, for at least 95% of requests, though these are "best practices and not guarantees". Nobody should charge you a fee, and you don't need to register first.

Verizon's form takes up to 50 numbers, or a file for more, and warns that a submission "may not result in a change to how future calls are labeled" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026). Send each number once, with a clear reason such as "my business calls people who asked us to call".

This is general information, not legal advice.

Calling habits that protect a number

If you change one thing, slow down: fewer dials per number per hour. You'll need more numbers or a longer day for the same volume, and we think it's worth it. These best practices each answer a signal above:

  1. Give each rep or team its own number, so the daily calls make smaller bursts on each one.
  2. Pause between calls, and if you ring several leads at once, keep the lines few.
  3. Let the phone ring. Hanging up after two rings makes the short, unanswered calls the analytics look for.
  4. Call in the lead's working hours, and run every list against your do-not-call list. Annoyed people tap report.
  5. Don't swap numbers to dodge a label. A fresh number called the same way gets the same label, so fix the habit, then dispute.
