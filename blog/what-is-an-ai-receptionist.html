What is an AI receptionist?

It's 7pm and you're in a customer's kitchen, quoting a job, when your phone buzzes for the third time. You let it ring, and the caller tries the next business on the list. An AI receptionist is software that picks up that call for you. It talks in a natural voice, answers from the information you've given it, and passes anything else to a person, with a transfer now or a callback in the morning.

CallForges team · 19 November 2026

Key takeaways
  • An AI receptionist answers calls in a natural voice, works from information you give it, and passes the rest to a person.
  • It earns its keep on calls you'd miss anyway, after hours and in the rush. Calls that need judgment or a calm human voice still go better with a person.
  • Callers give up on a bot that can't understand them or won't let them reach anyone, so pick one that says it's AI and puts them through when they ask.

How does an AI receptionist work?

You decide which calls it gets. Your call routing sends it the ones nobody else can take, say every call after 6pm, or any call your team doesn't pick up in time. Each call then runs the same way:

  1. It answers with your business name. A well-built one also says it's an automated assistant.
  2. Speech recognition turns what the caller says into text.
  3. A language model, the same kind of AI behind chat assistants, looks for the answer in the information you gave it and writes a short reply. A synthetic voice reads it out.
  4. If the answer isn't there, or the caller asks for a person, it transfers the call, takes a message or books a callback.

Its answers are only as good as what you give it: it knows your business as well as your documents do, from your opening hours to your price list. Say you raised your callout fee in March and never updated the FAQ. At 9pm, callers hear the old price.

What can an AI receptionist do, and where does it fall short?

It handles calls that have a written answer. It struggles when the answer depends on something nobody has seen yet, or on how the caller feels.

It handles wellBetter with a person
"Are you open Saturday?"A price for a job nobody has looked at
"Do you cover my area?"An angry customer with a complaint
Your callout fee, if you publish itA frightened caller with an emergency
Taking a name, a number and what the caller needsAnything you haven't written down

Quoting is where owners get burned. A developer posted about building an AI receptionist for a mechanic shop, and a former service advisor replied that a phone estimate is wrong unless the shop did that exact job not long ago, adding that "in some states, this matters a lot and will cost the shop money" (Hacker News, 2026). Give it your fixed fees, such as a callout or a diagnostic check, and leave other quotes to a person.

The other risk is a confident wrong answer, since a language model can say something false in a calm, sure voice. Two guards help: it answers only from your sources, and it books a callback instead of guessing. Before you trust it, call it with your ten most common questions and two you never wrote down, and listen to what it says.

Do callers hang up on an AI receptionist?

Some do. People hang up when the bot can't understand them, or when they can't get past it to a person.

In August 2026, a health watchdog in Rotherham, England, said patients with broad Yorkshire accents were struggling with the AI receptionist their GP practices had brought in. One patient "ended up just hanging up and not bothering to try and book an appointment" (The Guardian, 2026). Under the mechanic shop post, one commenter said they now avoid businesses that use one, after an AI receptionist couldn't understand their address and "couldn't forward" them to a human (Hacker News, 2026). Another liked a car dealership's AI assistant, because "there's always a human available if I need one" (Hacker News, 2026).

The dealership caller put up with a bot because a person was one request away. Have yours say it's AI in its first sentence, and let callers reach a person or leave a message at any point.

Does it have to say it's AI?

In the EU it does, and that's a law. Article 50 of the EU AI Act says the companies that build AI systems meant to talk with people must design them so people learn they're dealing with AI, unless that's obvious from the context. The system has to tell them at the latest at their first interaction (EU AI Act, Article 50), and the rule applies from 2 August 2026 (EU AI Act, Article 113). Elsewhere, check the rules where you and your callers are.

Wherever you are, we'd treat saying it up front as a best practice. It costs you one sentence, and a caller who wants a person can ask straight away.

This is general information, not legal advice.

AI receptionist vs a human receptionist or an answering service

An answering service is a call center whose operators answer with your greeting, follow a script you write and pass the messages on to you. The three suit different calls.

What it knowsWhat's in your documentsYour business, and more each monthYour script
Strong atRoutine questions and callbacks, in many languagesJudgment, quotes, upset callersA live voice for callers who won't talk to a bot
Weak atJudgment, reassurance, voices it can't followEvenings, weekends, two calls at onceQuestions outside the script
How you payPer minute it talksA wageIts own plan; ask how it counts minutes or calls

An AI receptionist fits when most calls have a written answer and many arrive when nobody's free, like the plumber under a sink. The trade-off: some callers won't talk to it, and it's only as good as your documents. Hire a person when calls need judgment, such as a quote or a frightened caller, and there's enough work to fill their day. Pick an answering service when your callers expect a live voice at night and you need messages taken.

Is an AI receptionist worth it?

It's worth it when the calls it catches would otherwise go to voicemail or a competitor. Count those first: check last month's call history for calls that rang out or went to voicemail, and the hours they came in.

Then run the numbers. As an example, say you miss 40 calls a month after hours, three minutes each. That's 120 agent minutes, or $14.40 at $0.12 a minute on Starter, on top of your plan. The US median pay for receptionists was $18.27 an hour in May 2025 (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, 2025), so one four-hour evening shift costs about $73 in wages alone. If one of those 40 callers turns into a $200 job, the month's minutes are paid for.

For most small teams we'd mix the two: people take calls during the day, and the AI receptionist takes the hours nobody's in, plus any call your team can't reach. We'd still send emergencies to a person on call. Someone with a burst pipe at 2am wants to hear a human say help is coming.
