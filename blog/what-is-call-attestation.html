What is call attestation? A, B and C explained

Call attestation is the level of trust a phone company puts on your caller ID when it signs a call under STIR/SHAKEN: A for full, B for partial and C for gateway. The company that puts your call on the network picks the letter, from what it knows about you and your right to use the number. Say your calling provider's welcome email says your calls go out "B-attested" until you finish a business form, and your sales manager wants to know what that costs you.

Key takeaways
  • The phone company that starts your call sets the level. You can't sign your own calls, and the network you're calling only checks the signature.
  • The gap between A and B is one check: whether the provider has confirmed you may use the number on your caller ID.
  • How it confirms that is each provider's own policy, so the same number can get A from one provider and B from another.

Who assigns attestation levels?

The phone company that puts your call on the network assigns the level when it signs the call. The FCC's rules call this authenticating the caller ID, "the process by which a voice service provider attests to the accuracy of caller identification information transmitted with a call it originates" (47 CFR 64.6300). The letter travels in the signed token's "attest" field (IETF RFC 8588, 2019).

The certificates that sign calls belong to phone companies. The industry's governance authority, the STI-GA, sets who may get one, and its criteria include an FCC Form 499-A on file, an Operating Company Number and a listing in the FCC's Robocall Mitigation Database (STI-GA SPC Token Access Policy, 2021). A business that makes calls has none of those, so its provider signs for it. Where a company sits in the call path caps the letter it can give:

Who signs the callHighest levelWhy
The provider that starts your callAIt knows its customer and can check the number
A company hired to do the signingNone of its ownIt signs with the provider's certificate, and the provider "makes all attestation-level decisions" (47 CFR 64.6301(b))
A US gateway taking a call from a foreign phone companyCIt must sign unsigned calls that show a US number, and it doesn't know the caller (47 CFR 64.6302)
A network in the middle that gets an unsigned callCOnly the provider that puts a call on the network may give it A or B

The FCC spelled out that last row in 2024: "an A- or B-level attestation can only be applied if the provider authenticating the call originates it onto the public network", and the customer check "cannot be made by a third party with no relationship to that customer" (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024). These rules apply to phone companies.

What decides whether a call gets A, B or C

The signing provider works through three conditions from the industry standard, ATIS-1000074. It must be responsible for putting the call on the network, it must have "a direct authenticated relationship with the customer and can identify the customer", and for A it must have "established a verified association with the telephone number used for the call" (FCC, 2024). Pass the first two and the call gets B (our STIR/SHAKEN guide puts the levels side by side).

Calls drop from A to B on the third condition, and the standard leaves it to each provider: the numbers a customer may use are "subject to signer-specific policy". It lists four ways a provider can settle it (STI-GA, Improper Attestation, 2023):

  • The provider assigned the number to this customer.
  • The number is part of a range assigned to the customer, such as a company's block of numbers.
  • The provider has made sure the customer may use the number, by a business agreement or evidence, even for a number from another provider.
  • The number isn't the customer's for good, but the provider can track who used it on which calls.

The same note warns that a provider's "reputation may be directly dependent on how rigorous they have been" (same source), so a stricter provider may sign B where another signs A. A B still carries a promise: the provider attests "that it can trace the source of the call to a customer for policy enforcement purposes" (same source).

Which level your calls get, case by case

Most business calls fit one of four setups. Your provider's policy decides the letter, so treat these as likely results:

Your setupLikely levelWhat gets you to A
A number from your calling provider, which has verified your businessAYou're there
A number from a calling platform that hasn't verified your business yetBFinish the platform's business registration
Your office line from another phone company, shown as caller IDB, unless the provider accepts your proofAsk what proof it takes, or move the number over
A foreign phone company carries your calls into the US with a US caller IDCCall through a US provider that knows your business

A gateway signs calls handed over by a foreign phone company (47 CFR 64.6300), so a rep in London who calls through a US provider's app isn't in the last row.

What B costs you in pickups varies, because each called network decides how much weight the letter gets. We'd still call from numbers your calling provider gives you and register your business with it. Keeping the old office line as caller ID feels familiar, and it's an easy way to stay stuck on B. If customers must keep seeing that number, port it to the provider you call through.

What happens when a provider signs A it shouldn't

The FCC can fine it, and the industry can take away its right to sign. In January 2024 a voice provider signed 3,978 calls to potential New Hampshire voters at level A. The calls used a spoofed number and a fake voice made to sound like President Biden. The provider had relied on its long-time customer's written promise that the numbers were verified, and took no extra steps to check for itself. In August 2024 it agreed to pay $1,000,000 and to sign A only on numbers it had provided to the caller itself (FCC consent decree, 2024).

The STI-GA counts as improper any level "inconsistent with the information it has, or is required to have, about the call", such as an A on an illegally spoofed call. Its revocation process can take away a provider's SPC token, which the provider needs for its certificate (STI-GA, 2023).

That's why your provider asks for your business details before it signs A, and we'd count it in the provider's favor: one that signs A for anyone puts at risk the token all its customers' calls are signed with.

Questions about call attestation

Can I choose my call attestation level?

No. Your provider decides, even when another company does the signing for it. What you can do is give it what A needs: who your business is, and proof you may use the number.

How do I test a call's attestation level?

Ask the provider that signs your calls, since it picks the level. A test call to your own phone won't settle it, because what a phone shows for a verified call depends on its network and handset. In CallForges, the caller verification card marks a number Approved once its calls are signed at A.

Is attestation the same as STIR/SHAKEN verification?

They're the two ends of one call. Attestation happens when your provider signs the call, and verification happens when the called person's network checks the signature (47 CFR 64.6300).

Does A attestation stop spam labels?

No. The called network weighs the signature along with how your number calls, so a busy number signed at A can still be labeled. Phone number reputation covers that half.
