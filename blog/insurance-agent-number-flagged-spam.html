Insurance agents: why your number gets flagged as spam

Insurance agents' numbers get flagged because the call analytics behind spam labels judge how a number calls, and insurance calling often looks like a robocall run: many dials from one number, short unanswered calls to aged leads, double dials and spam reports. Robocallers use insurance heavily too. Change the pattern, register your numbers and watch each number's pick-up rate.

Why do insurance agents' numbers get flagged as spam?

Because the call analytics of the called person's network, or of an app on their phone, rate a number by how it calls, and insurance calling sets off the same signals as the robocalls those systems were built to stop. How that rating works in general is in our guide to phone number reputation: bursts of calls, short or unanswered calls, and reports from people who did not want the call all pull it down.

Insurance adds a second problem: the company it keeps. The FCC reported that about 23.6 million health insurance robocalls were crossing the networks of the four largest wireless carriers each day, when it proposed the largest fine in its history against one health insurance telemarketing operation that made about 1 billion spoofed robocalls in less than five months (FCC, 2020). It imposed the $225 million fine the next year (FCC Forfeiture Order, 2021). That is the traffic spam labels were built to catch. An agency calling about health, Medicare or life cover rings the same phones about the same kind of product, so it has to look different in how it calls.

Agents talk about it. On insurance-forums.com, one asks for help with a number getting flagged as spam, and the replies turn to how many calls a day go out from a single line. Another thread is titled simply "Scam likely?".

Which kinds of insurance calling trip the labels?

The calling that pays in insurance is often the calling that looks worst to the analytics: old lead lists, high dial counts per agent and seasons when everyone calls at once.

Aged leads hurt twice. About 35 million US numbers are disconnected and made available for reassignment each year (FCC Second Report and Order, 2018), so part of a year-old list rings strangers who never asked for a quote, and they decline or report the call. A label on the agency's main line also lands on the calls clients most need to pick up: the claim follow-up and the renewal reminder.

Which calling rules matter for an insurance agent's number?

Three sets of rules shape how an agent may call: the FCC's telemarketing rules, Medicare's marketing rules for Medicare Advantage agents, and state laws. They matter for your number too, because the call that breaks one is usually the call that gets reported. Here is how they read in November 2026:

Why number rotation and spoofing backfire

Swapping a labeled number for a fresh one and calling the same way only buys time, and showing a number you don't own can break the law:

  • The new number is judged the same way. Same list, same dial count, same short calls: the analytics see the same pattern, and the new number goes the way of the old one.
  • The rules follow the person, not the number. Florida's limit counts calls "regardless of the phone number used to make the call", and a "don't call me" applies to your agency on every number it has.
  • Callbacks stop reaching you. Leads call back the number they saw, and the FCC's caller ID rule says it must take a do-not-call request during business hours. A number you dropped last week may ring nobody.
  • A borrowed number can't be verified, and its owner gets your angry callbacks. Level A, the highest SHAKEN/STIR level, means the carrier knows your business and its right to the number, so a number you don't control can't get it. In the FCC's $225 million case, the companies whose numbers were spoofed were "overwhelmed with angry call-backs" (FCC, 2020).

Resting a number while you fix the cause is different: you stop the pattern, clear the label and bring the number back at a human pace.

What to do when your agency's number is flagged

Find what changed in how the number calls and fix that first, then register the number and dispute the label. The industry's own guidance says to look at your calling habits before you file a dispute (USTelecom, 2026), and our guide shows how to check and dispute a label.

  1. Confirm it. Call phones on a few different networks from the number and see what their screens show.
  2. Find what changed in the last two weeks: dials per number per day, a new aged-lead list, double dials, hang-ups after two rings, late evening calls.
  3. Clean the list. Scrub it against the national Do Not Call Registry, take out everyone on your own do-not-call list, and drop numbers that no longer belong to the lead. The FCC's reassigned numbers database tells callers whether a number was permanently disconnected since the date they got consent (47 CFR 64.1200(m)).
  4. Slow the number down. Fewer calls a day, let each call ring, and one call per attempt instead of a double dial.
  5. Register your business and the number with the carriers, with your real use case and call volume.
  6. Dispute the label with the network that shows it. Our reputation guide lists where.
