How leasing calls go wrong

Renters call while they browse, and they browse after work and on weekends, when your leasing office is closed or out on tours. Voicemail doesn't hold them. Someone with ten saved listings calls the next one, so we'd count each missed evening call as a lead you may have lost.

The questions repeat. On a busy Saturday your leasing agent might answer "is it still available?" a dozen times, often in the middle of a tour. The rest follow close behind:

  • Do you take pets, and what's the pet rent?
  • Where do I park, and does it cost extra?
  • What are the fees, and how do I apply?
  • Can I see it this week?

Maintenance calls land on the same line, so a leak and a parking question end up in one voicemail box, and someone plays every message to find the one that can't wait. An AI voice agent takes the routine calls off that pile, and your after-hours answering plan decides who gets the urgent ones.

Fair housing: the agent says what you wrote

An agent gives every caller the same answer, at 9pm or 9am, whoever they sound like. That helps with fair housing, but one bad line in a source gets read to everyone. These federal laws and regulations apply to landlords, property managers and leasing agents:

RuleWhat it says (November 2026)
Fair Housing Act (DOJ)Bars landlords and other housing providers from discriminating because of race, disability, familial status and the other traits it protects.
Statements (24 CFR 100.75)No notice or statement that shows a preference or limitation based on those traits. It covers "all written or oral" statements, so phone answers count.
Availability (24 CFR 100.80)No inaccurate information about whether a unit is available, given because of one of those traits.
Reasonable accommodations (24 CFR 100.204)Make reasonable exceptions to your rules for a person with a disability. HUD's own examples: a seeing eye dog in a no-pets building, and a reserved space near the unit for a tenant who can't walk far.

Pets and parking come up on leasing calls all day, and HUD picked both as examples. Before you give the agent a source, read it the way you'd read a listing ad:

  • The pet policy says how you handle assistance animals, so "no pets" never reaches a caller as the whole answer.
  • The parking policy leaves room for accessibility requests.
  • The text describes the unit. Phrases like "adults only" or "no kids" signal a preference about familial status and don't belong in any source.
  • Someone owns the availability page and updates it the day a unit is rented.

State and local laws often protect more groups than the federal act, so check yours too. If you text prospects, a reply such as "stop" revokes consent to automated texts, and you have 10 business days at most to honor it (47 CFR 64.1200(a)(10)).

This is general information, not legal advice.
