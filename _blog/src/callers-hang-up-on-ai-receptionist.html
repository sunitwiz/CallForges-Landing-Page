Say you run a locksmith business, and on Monday you scroll the weekend's calls: a row of them ended a few seconds after your AI receptionist picked up. Chances are those callers thought they were stuck with a machine. Callers hang up on an AI receptionist when they can't see a way to a person, and when it can't help them. Hiding that it's AI, or talking too long before it helps, makes both worse. Each cause has a fix you control.

Key takeaways
  • Many hang-ups come in the first seconds, when a caller decides whether they're trapped.
  • Say it's an automated assistant and how to reach a person in the opening lines. In one 30-day test, frustrated hang-ups fell once callers heard how to reach a person.
  • Keep the greeting short, answer only from your own sources, and book a callback instead of guessing.

What callers and owners say

A developer's post about building an AI receptionist for a mechanic shop drew more than 300 comments (Hacker News, 2026). Plenty came from callers explaining when they'd hang up. One wrote that a bot repeating what's already on the website "would probably frustrate me enough to hang up and call a different shop immediately" (Hacker News, 2026). Another wrote: "What really sucks (AI or decision tree, either way) is when they don't let you easily speak with someone" (Hacker News, 2026).

Owners see it in their call logs. A consultant tested an AI receptionist for 30 days on a client's line, a small boiler and heating repair firm in Kent, England. It took 214 calls, and 41 callers hung up within 10 seconds of hearing it wasn't a human (I Tried an AI Phone Receptionist for 30 Days, 2026). The same test found two changes that brought that number down. (What is an AI receptionist? covers how one handles a call.)

They can't see a way to a person

Callers have spent years pressing zero in phone menus, so a bot sounds like the start of another maze, and some leave before it has said anything useful. In the Kent test, once the tester told callers to "press 1 or say 'speak to a person' at any time," frustrated hang-ups "dropped noticeably, even on days with no engineer free to take the call."

The fix has three parts:

  • Say how to reach a person in the greeting, in plain words: "Say 'a person' at any time."
  • Send that request somewhere real. In open hours, that's the team that handles the call. At night, it's whoever is on call.
  • Make sure someone works through the callbacks the agent books, first thing in the morning.

The calls you give the agent matter too. Start it on calls that would otherwise reach voicemail, after hours and when your team is busy. A caller who'd have heard a beep has less reason to leave.

It hides that it's AI, or talks before it helps

A bot that pretends to be a person tends to get found out, and the caller feels tricked. The Kent test tried a version that passed itself off as a regular receptionist: "callers who twigged got annoyed and hung up mid-sentence far more often than when it was upfront." Being straight about what it was "reduced the hang-up rate over the second half of the month." A commenter on the mechanic shop thread was blunter: "Automated voice systems that try to sound human but are in fact purely scripted are insanely annoying" (Hacker News, 2026).

Length is the other trap. A greeting that lists every service, or a bot that wants your name, address and order number before it answers, wears callers down. One commenter hung up on an automated line "because it kept redirecting the conversation," pushing for more detail than a simple question needed (Hacker News, 2026).

Keep the greeting to two sentences. The first says who they've reached and that it's an automated assistant. The second says what it can help with and how to reach a person. For example:

"Hi, you've reached Northside Locks. I'm an automated assistant and can help with our hours, prices and service areas. Say 'a person' at any time to reach our team."

Then let the caller talk. Ask for a name and number only when the agent books a callback or passes the call on. How to set up an AI receptionist covers the greeting with the rest of the setup.

It can't answer, or can't understand the caller

A bot that only reads out the website gives callers nothing they couldn't find themselves, which is what the commenter who'd ring another shop objected to. The commenter who found scripted bots annoying said one "that knows what it knows and tells you when it doesn't" could work well.

Give the agent the answers people ring for: your hours, the areas you cover, the fees you publish and your policies on cancellations or deposits. For anything else, it should book a callback, not make something up. A confident wrong price is worse than a hang-up, because the caller believes it. AI receptionist making things up covers how to keep its answers right.

Understanding is the other half. A caller with a strong accent, a caller who speaks another language, or one ringing from a windy roadside may not get through to the agent the first time. The fixes:

  • Use an agent that answers in the caller's own language.
  • Test it with real voices before you rely on it: colleagues with different accents, a call from a car, a call from a busy street.
  • Keep a person one request away, so anyone it can't follow still gets help.
