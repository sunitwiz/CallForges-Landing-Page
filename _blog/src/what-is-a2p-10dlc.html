You set up texting on your new US business number, send appointment reminders to twenty customers, and none of them arrive. A2P 10DLC registration is the reason. US carriers block business texts from local numbers they haven't approved, so a business has to register itself and describe what it will send before its number can text. Once the carriers approve that registration, your texts go through.

Key takeaways
  • A2P 10DLC is the US carriers' sign-up process for businesses that text from ordinary local numbers.
  • You register your business and a campaign: what you'll send, to whom, and how people agreed to get it.
  • Expect carrier fees and a review of one to three weeks, so register before you need to text.

What A2P and 10DLC stand for

A2P means application-to-person: texts a business sends from software, like a reminder, a reply from your team inbox or a follow-up after a missed call. The opposite is person-to-person, the texts you send friends from your own phone.

10DLC means 10-digit long code. That's an ordinary US local number, like (512) 555-0134, as opposed to a short number built for mass texting or a toll-free number. Put together, A2P 10DLC is business texting from a normal local number.

Registration is how the carriers know who is behind those texts. You tell them about your business (the "brand") and about each kind of texting you'll do (a "campaign"). They review both, then let your numbers send.

Why carriers make you register

Registration is carrier policy, not a law. No statute says "register for 10DLC", but the US carriers block business texts from unregistered local numbers, so in practice you can't skip it.

The carriers' rules grow out of the industry's own guidance. The CTIA Messaging Principles, which the wireless trade group calls voluntary best practices developed by its member companies, cover texting from 10-digit numbers (CTIA, 2023). They expect a business to get a person's consent before texting them, written consent for marketing, and to let people opt out.

The point of registering is accountability. A spammer who has to give a tax ID, sample messages and a description of how people opted in is easier to trace and cut off, and an honest business gets a known, approved way to text.

What you submit

A 10DLC registration has two parts. The brand is your business: its legal name, tax ID and contact details, checked once. The campaign is one kind of texting, and you can have more than one.

Part of the campaignWhat the carriers want to see
Use caseWhat the texts are for, such as customer care, marketing or account notifications
Sample messagesReal examples, with your business name and how to opt out, like "Reply STOP to opt out"
ConsentHow people agree to get your texts, such as a web form, and where it is
DescriptionWhat you send, to whom and why, in plain words
NumbersThe local numbers that will send these texts

The consent part is where most of the care should go. The CTIA guidance treats a conversation the customer starts as consent for your replies, and expects written agreement before you send promotional texts (CTIA, 2023). If your form doesn't say people will get texts from you, fix the form before you register.

This is general information, not legal advice.

Who needs 10DLC registration?

Any business texting from a US local number does. That includes a two-person plumbing company sending "we're on our way" texts. A few cases don't need it:

  • Calls. Registration is about texts, and your calls work the same either way.
  • Numbers outside the US. A UK or Australian number follows its own country's rules, and many countries need no registration.
  • Toll-free numbers, which follow a separate process.
