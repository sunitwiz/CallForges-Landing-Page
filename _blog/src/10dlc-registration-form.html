Before the form: your business details

The carriers check your business before they read a word of your campaign. What is A2P 10DLC registration? explains the brand and the campaign. In CallForges, the brand is registered with your first campaign, from your approved Business profile in the Compliance center.

The registry that runs 10DLC checks your EIN, legal name and address against independent sources. Its guide says the most common reason a business fails that check is a legal name and EIN that don't match what's on file with the federal government (10DLC registry user guide, 2026). It tells you to copy the name from your IRS EIN confirmation letter, first line only, and to use the address you registered with the IRS.

So "Bright Plumbing" fails if your letter says "Bright Plumbing Services LLC". On the Business profile, fill in General information and Business information to match that letter, add a Point of contact, then click Submit for review. Approval usually takes 24 to 48 hours.

Campaign details: use case, numbers and volume

Open the Compliance center, find Text messaging (A2P 10DLC) and click Add campaign. You need a role that can Buy and release numbers. The first step, Campaign details, asks for a campaign name, the use case and the numbers. Name the campaign after its texts, like "Appointment reminders", so your team can tell campaigns apart.

Choose the use case your samples will prove: Customer care for replies and service updates, Account notifications for alerts about an account, Marketing for promotions. A Mixed or Low volume mixed campaign takes two to five sub use cases. We'd keep one purpose per campaign; a workspace can have up to 5, so reminders and promotions can live in two. The industry's guidance says a call to action such as a coupon can turn an informational text into a promotional one (CTIA, 2023), so a discount code in a Customer care sample tells the reviewer your campaign is something else.

The numbers list shows only your US numbers that are in no other campaign, and each number belongs to one campaign. Toll-free numbers can't be registered here.

Your first campaign also asks How many texts will you send a day? Under 2,000 a day is the default and registers your business without vetting, which suits most small teams. More than 2,000 a day adds business vetting: an independent firm checks your business and gives it a trust score that sets your daily limit. Later campaigns don't ask again.

Before you click Continue, you accept the content carriers don't allow, such as cannabis, high-risk loans or gambling. If your texts touch one of those, no wording elsewhere on the form will get the campaign through.

Sample messages: the texts you'll send

Sample messages takes two to five texts, 20 to 1,024 characters each, written as your contacts will get them. Name your business in each one and add "Reply STOP to opt out". Two for a plumbing company:

  • "Hi Dana, it's Bright Plumbing. Mike is booked for Thursday between 2 and 4pm at 18 Oak St. Reply here to change the time. Reply STOP to opt out."
  • "Bright Plumbing: Mike is on his way and should arrive in about 20 minutes. Reply STOP to opt out."

Both read as Customer care. Use a real name and time in each, so the reviewer sees the text the way it lands on a phone.

The step also asks whether your texts are about lending money, or contain links or phone numbers. Answer yes if any text ever will. For links, use your own domain: the registry's guide says public link shorteners aren't accepted (10DLC registry user guide, 2026), and the CTIA expects a link to show whose website it is.

User consent: show how people agreed

User consent is the step we'd spend the most time on. In 40 to 2,048 characters, describe how contacts agree to get your texts, such as a web form and its address. The registry's guide says the opt-in must make the program clear, and if people can opt in more than one way, you list every way (10DLC registry user guide, 2026).

Under the CTIA's guidance, the opt-in itself should tell people what the texts are about, who sends them and from which number, any fees, how to opt out, and where your privacy policy is (CTIA, 2023). A checkbox on a booking form might read:

"By checking this box, you agree to get appointment texts from Bright Plumbing, sent from (512) 555-0134 to the number above. Msg and data rates may apply. Reply STOP to opt out, HELP for help. See our Privacy Policy."

Then write the step so a stranger could find it: "Customers book at brightplumbing.com/book and tick an unchecked box that reads..." followed by that wording. A bought or shared list doesn't count. The same guidance says senders should build their own opt-in lists, and it expects written consent, such as that ticked box, before marketing texts. Opt-in keywords such as START, with the reply contacts get, are optional.

The CTIA's principles are voluntary best practice for any business or organization that texts consumers, not a law. The registry's guide points to them for building a compliant campaign, and federal and state laws on texting consent still apply. Check your forms with counsel.

Description, review and the fees

Description is what you send, to whom and why, in 40 to 4,096 characters. Write it as a summary of the steps before it: "Bright Plumbing texts customers who book a repair online: booking confirmations, arrival times and answers to their questions. Customers opt in with a checkbox on our booking form." The carriers read it next to your samples and your consent, so all three should describe the same texts.
