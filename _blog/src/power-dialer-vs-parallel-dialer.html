Monday morning, and you've got two lists: 400 cold business contacts, most of whom won't pick up, and 60 people who booked a demo and didn't show. Put the cold list on a parallel dialer and the no-shows on a power dialer. A power dialer rings one lead at a time, so you're on the line when anyone answers. A parallel dialer rings several at once and puts you through to the first who picks up, and it hangs up on the rest.

Key takeaways
  • A power dialer rings one lead at a time, so everyone who answers reaches you. A parallel dialer rings several and connects the first person who picks up.
  • We'd use the parallel dialer on big cold business lists where most calls ring out, and the power dialer for warm leads and for calls to consumers.
  • When two leads answer at once on a parallel dialer, the second one is hung up on. Abandoned-call rules are written about that call.

When the power dialer is the better pick

Pick it when your leads tend to answer, or when you call consumers. People who asked for a call or already buy from you pick up often, and on a parallel dialer every extra answer is one more chance that two people answer together.

You start the campaign and it calls the lead at the top of your list from your own line. You hear it ring, and you're there the moment they say hello, with their contact page open. When you hang up, a short countdown gives you time for notes before the next lead rings. Reach a voicemail box and one click leaves your recorded message, then the dialer moves on.

You also decide who comes next. The list runs top to bottom in the order you set, so your best accounts go first and you can read up on each one before it rings.

The annoying part is the waiting. Say nine in ten calls on a cold list ring out: you sit through every one of those rings and voicemail greetings in a row. That's the list the parallel dialer is for.

When the parallel dialer is the better pick

Pick it for big cold business lists, where most calls ring out, hit a busy line or go to voicemail. On those lists a one-line dialer spends most of your call block on misses, and ringing several leads at once runs those misses side by side.

Your own line connects first, so the dialer rings leads only when you're ready to talk. It then rings the next few leads on the list and refills any line that ends without an answer. The first person to pick up is put through to you with their contact page open, and the dialer hangs up every other line. If you've recorded a voicemail drop and turned on machine detection, an answering machine hears your message and the dialer moves on without you.

The cost is the leads it cuts. As an example, take 300 leads where one in ten picks up, so about 30 conversations. On three lines, each of those can cut up to two other leads mid-ring: up to 60 people who heard a ring and then nothing. Now and then two people answer at the same moment, and the second one hears the line go dead. By default the dialer won't call a cut lead again in that campaign.

We'd start at two lines and move to three once you've seen how many leads a session cuts. Past three, we'd want a list that's mostly voicemail and dead numbers. How to set up a parallel dialer goes through the line count in detail.

Which dialer is easier to keep compliant?

The power dialer. You're on the line for every call it places, so the person who answers reaches you. A parallel dialer makes the call the rules target each time two leads answer together, because the second one is hung up with no message.

  • The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule, a US law, counts a call as abandoned when the person who answers isn't connected to a sales rep within two seconds of their greeting. Its safe harbor allows at most 3 percent of answered calls per campaign, measured over each 30-day period, and expects a recorded message with the seller's name and phone number when nobody is free (16 CFR 310.4, eCFR 2026). Most business-to-business calls are exempt from the TSR.
  • The FCC's rule, also a US law, sets the same 3 percent limit on abandoned telemarketing calls over 30 days, with the same two seconds, and its text has no business-to-business exemption (47 CFR 64.1200, eCFR 2026).
  • Ofcom's policy on persistent misuse (2016) is a UK regulator's policy: how Ofcom says it will use its powers under the Communications Act 2003. It counts a call hung up as or just after the person picks up, with no message, as a silent call, and it treats silent calls as even more harmful than abandoned ones. It sets out no exemption for calls to businesses.

Our own rule of thumb is a best practice: consumer lists go on the power dialer, and the parallel dialer stays on business lists with a few lines. Check your lists with counsel either way.
