Key takeaways
  • Networks and spam apps label your number by how it calls, and insurance shares those phones with a flood of robocalls.
  • Old lead lists and double dials look like robocalls, and people report the calls that break the rules.

Why do insurance agents' numbers get flagged as spam?

Call analytics at the called person's network or in a phone app score your number by how it calls: bursts of dials pull it down, and so do short unanswered calls and spam reports.

You start with a handicap in insurance, because robocallers pitch it too. The FCC counted about 23.6 million health insurance robocalls a day crossing the four largest US wireless networks (FCC, 2020) when it proposed the biggest fine in its history, against a health insurance telemarketer that made about 1 billion spoofed robocalls in under five months. It imposed the $225 million fine the next year (FCC Forfeiture Order, 2021). You ring the same phones about the same products, so your calling has to look different.

Agents on insurance-forums.com start threads like "Scam likely?" and ask for help with a number getting flagged as spam.

Aged leads, double dials and AEP

Start with aged leads. Carriers disconnect about 35 million US numbers a year and make them available to new owners (FCC Second Report and Order, 2018), so part of a year-old list rings strangers who never asked for a quote. They decline, or they tap report.

We'd drop the double dial first. Some leads grab the second ring, which is why agents do it, but say you double dial 200 leads in an afternoon: that's up to 400 calls from one number, half to people who let the first ring out. You lose a few late pickups and keep a number clients still answer.

In AEP you take a number that sat quiet all summer to full volume overnight. Medicare.gov calls it the Open Enrollment Period, October 15 to December 7 (Medicare.gov, checked 2026). Ramp the number up in the weeks before.

In P&C, if X-date calls go out from the main line, clients see the label on your claim follow-ups too.

Why rotating or spoofing numbers backfires

Sooner or later someone on the floor says to buy a stack of new numbers and spread the dials. We wouldn't. A fresh number calling the same list the same way ends up with the same label. Rest the number while you fix the pattern, then bring it back at a human pace.

The rules still apply to your agency. Florida counts calls "regardless of the phone number used", and a "don't call me" covers every number you have. Leads also call back the number they saw, and a dropped number can't take their call or their do-not-call request.

Showing someone else's number is worse. It can't get level A, the top SHAKEN/STIR level, which needs the carrier to know your business and its right to the number. And its owner gets your callbacks: in the FCC's $225 million case, the companies whose numbers the telemarketer spoofed were "overwhelmed with angry call-backs" (FCC, 2020).

What to do when your number is flagged

USTelecom's best practices for redress requests (2026) say to review your calling habits before you dispute a label.

  1. Confirm the label by calling phones on a few networks from the number, then check what changed in the last two weeks, like a new aged-lead list or more dials per number.
  2. Scrub the list against the national Do Not Call Registry and your own list, and drop numbers that changed hands: the FCC's reassigned numbers database shows whether a number was permanently disconnected since you got consent (47 CFR 64.1200(m)).
  3. Make fewer dials a day and let each call ring out instead of double dialing.
  4. Register the business and the number, then dispute the label with the network that shows it, using our guide to checking and disputing a label.
