Someone on your sales floor says calls from the new number go unanswered "because of STIR/SHAKEN", and nobody's sure what that means. It's how US phone companies sign each call, so the network at the other end can check that the caller ID is real. Calls are signed at level A, B or C. Level A, the highest, means your phone company knows your business and has verified your link to the number.

Key takeaways
  • Your phone company signs each call as it leaves, and the called person's network checks the signature when the call arrives.
  • Level A means the phone company knows you and has verified your link to the number. Level B skips the number check, and level C only says where the call came in.
  • The FCC's rules bind phone companies, not callers, and a signed call can still be labeled spam because networks also weigh how your number calls.

How a call gets signed

Your phone company puts its digital signature on the caller ID, and the receiving network decrypts it to see who signed and what they vouched for. The FCC describes the framework as industry standards "allowing authenticated caller ID information to securely travel with the call itself throughout the entire call path" (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024).

The name joins two standards. STIR is the set of protocols written by the Internet Engineering Task Force, and SHAKEN is the specification the US phone industry wrote for using them across networks (same source). On a call it runs in three steps:

  1. Your phone company gets a certificate through a neutral governance system. The certificate says the company is who it claims to be and may sign caller ID.
  2. When you call, your phone company adds a signed header with its certificate and an attestation level: how well it knows you and your number.
  3. The called person's network checks the signature and uses it, "along with other information", to protect its customers from unwanted and illegal calls (same source).

What do attestation levels A, B and C mean?

The level says how much the signing phone company vouches for you. A is "full", B is "partial" and C is "gateway" (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024).

LevelThe signing phone companyWhat the receiving network learns about you
A, "full"Started the call, knows the customer, and has "established a verified association with the telephone number used for the call"Who sent the call, and that the number belongs with that caller
B, "partial"Started the call and knows the customer, but has not verified the numberWho sent the call, but not that the number is theirs
C, "gateway"Is only "the entry point of the call into its VoIP network" with "no relationship with the initiator of the call", as is typical for calls from abroadWhere the call came in, and nothing about the caller

We'd want level A on every number a sales team calls from, because it's the most a phone company can vouch for. Each receiving network decides how much weight a level gets, so the effect varies by carrier.

Who has to use STIR/SHAKEN?

Phone companies do. These are FCC rules made under the TRACED Act of 2019, and they bind the companies that carry your calls, so you never sign a call yourself. The level your phone company can give depends on what it knows about you and your numbers (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024):

WhoWhat the FCC requires
Phone companies that start callsSign calls in the IP parts of their networks, since June 30, 2021
Gateway providers, which bring calls from abroad into the USSign unsigned calls that show a US number, since June 30, 2023
Networks in the middlePass on any signature they receive unaltered, and since December 31, 2023, sign an unsigned call that comes straight from the phone company that started it
Every provider, signing or notFile in the FCC's Robocall Mitigation Database. Networks may not accept calls directly from a provider that isn't listed there.

STIR/SHAKEN only works on IP-based voice networks, so providers that still rely on older equipment have an ongoing extension. The FCC also polices level A. In 2024 it proposed a $2,000,000 penalty against a phone company over calls it had signed at level A, and it set rules that keep a phone company responsible for its signatures when another company does the technical signing (same source).

This is general information, not legal advice.

What a signature can't do for you

A signature doesn't decide whether your call is answered, labeled or blocked. The receiving network weighs it with everything else it knows about your number, and that part is each carrier's own policy.

Verizon told the FCC its call filter uses "a call's STIR/SHAKEN verification results as one component of the more holistic real-time analysis to determine whether to block or label a call" (FCC Second Report on Call Blocking, 2021). Its spam feedback form says it does not label a call "based solely on caller ID authentication information" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026). Say a rep makes 250 dials a day from one number and most ring out unanswered. Level A tells the network who's calling, and the analytics still see a number that calls like a robocaller. That's phone number reputation, and it's a separate fix.

A signature doesn't vouch for the caller's intentions either. AT&T's own page says that "even if AT&T validates the number, people can still use valid numbers for bad purposes" (AT&T, 2025).

It doesn't put your business name on the screen. Level A is about your number, and the name comes from a separate system, caller ID name (CNAM). Any visible sign of the signature depends on the person's network and phone. AT&T's business phone service, for example, puts a [V] in front of a call it has verified (same source).
