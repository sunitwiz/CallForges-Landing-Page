STIR/SHAKEN is the system US phone companies use to sign calls, so the network receiving a call can check that the caller ID is real. Each call is signed at level A, B or C. Level A, the highest, means your phone company knows your business and has verified your link to the number.

Key takeaways
  • Your phone company signs the call as it leaves, and the called person's network checks the signature when it arrives.
  • Level A means the phone company started the call, knows you and verified your link to the number. Level B skips the number check, and level C only says where the call entered the network.
  • The rules apply to phone companies, not to you as a caller. The FCC required them to sign calls on their IP networks by June 30, 2021.
  • A signature is one input. Networks still weigh how your number calls, so a level A call can still be labeled spam.

What is STIR/SHAKEN?

STIR/SHAKEN is a set of industry standards that lets a phone company sign the caller ID on each call, so the network at the other end can check it. The FCC describes it as "allowing authenticated caller ID information to securely travel with the call itself throughout the entire call path" (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024).

The name joins two standards: STIR, the protocols written by the Internet Engineering Task Force, and SHAKEN, the specification the US phone industry wrote for using them across networks (same source). In practice it works in three steps:

  1. Your phone company gets a certificate through a neutral governance system. The certificate says the company is who it claims to be and may sign caller ID.
  2. When you call, your phone company adds a signed header to the call with its certificate and an attestation level: how well it knows you and your number.
  3. The called person's network checks the signature and uses it, "along with other information", to protect its customers from unwanted and illegal calls (same source).

What do attestation levels A, B and C mean?

The attestation level says how much the signing phone company vouches for the caller. A is "full", B is "partial" and C is "gateway", and a sales team wants level A on every number it calls from (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024).

LevelThe signing phone companyWhat the receiving network learns about you
A, "full"Started the call, knows the customer, and has "established a verified association with the telephone number used for the call"Who sent the call, and that the number really belongs with that caller
B, "partial"Started the call and knows the customer, but has not verified the numberWho sent the call, but not that the number is theirs
C, "gateway"Is only "the entry point of the call into its VoIP network" with "no relationship with the initiator of the call", as is typical for calls from abroadWhere the call came in, and nothing about the caller

Each network decides how much weight to give a level. Level A is the most a phone company can vouch for, which is why it is the level to aim for.

Who has to use STIR/SHAKEN?

Phone companies do, not callers. You never sign calls yourself: your phone company signs them for you, and the level it can give depends on what it knows about you and your numbers. These are the FCC's rules (FCC Eighth Report and Order, 2024):

  • Phone companies that start calls. Following the TRACED Act of 2019, the FCC required them to use STIR/SHAKEN in the IP parts of their networks by June 30, 2021.
  • Gateway providers, which bring calls from abroad into the US. Since June 30, 2023 they must sign unsigned calls that show a US number.
  • Networks in the middle. One that receives an unsigned call straight from the phone company that started it must sign it, since December 31, 2023. All of them must pass on a signature they receive unaltered.
  • Every provider, whether it signs calls or not, must file in the FCC's Robocall Mitigation Database. The FCC's rules bar networks from accepting calls directly from a provider that is not listed there.

There are limits. STIR/SHAKEN only works on IP-based voice networks, so networks that still rely on older equipment have an ongoing extension. The FCC also polices level A: in 2024 it proposed a $2,000,000 penalty against a phone company over calls it had signed at level A, and it set rules that keep a phone company responsible for its signatures even when another company does the technical signing (same source).

This is general information, not legal advice.

What does STIR/SHAKEN not do?

It does not decide by itself whether your call is answered, labeled or blocked. It tells the receiving network who signed the call and how well they know you, and the network weighs that with everything else it knows about your number.

  • It does not remove a spam label. Verizon told the FCC its call filter uses "a call's STIR/SHAKEN verification results as one component of the more holistic real-time analysis to determine whether to block or label a call" (FCC Second Report on Call Blocking, 2021). Its spam feedback form says it does not label a call "based solely on caller ID authentication information" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026). A level A call can still be labeled when the number calls like a robocaller.
  • It does not prove the caller is honest. AT&T puts it plainly: "even if AT&T validates the number, people can still use valid numbers for bad purposes" (AT&T, 2025).
  • It does not show your name. Level A is about your number, not your business name. In CallForges your name shows only with an approved caller ID name, a separate registration.
  • It is not always visible. Whether the person sees anything depends on their network and phone. AT&T's business phone service, for example, puts a [V] in front of a call it has verified (same source).

Frequently asked questions

Do I need STIR/SHAKEN to call US numbers?

No. Your phone company signs your calls, and you don't have to register anything to make them. In CallForges, calls to US phones work without a Business profile, including on the free trial, and are signed at level B. Registering moves them to level A.

Will level A stop my calls showing as spam?

Not by itself. It is the most your phone company can vouch for, but networks weigh it with how your number calls and with spam reports. Phone number reputation, a separate registration in the same Compliance center, registers your numbers with the carriers to lower that risk.
