A founder and two reps load 2,000 contacts into a predictive dialer that promises to keep all three talking all day. Then a lead answers, nobody is free, and the lead hears a recording or nothing. For a team that size, we'd use a power dialer: it rings one lead per rep, so the rep is there for every answer. A predictive dialer rings more leads than you have free agents, and that only works on a big floor.

Key takeaways
  • A power dialer rings one lead per rep. A predictive dialer rings several per agent and drops, or plays a message to, the people nobody is free to take.
  • Predictive dialing needs a crowd. Its guesses hold up with dozens of agents on the floor and fall apart with a handful of reps.
  • The FTC names predictive dialers as a common cause of abandoned calls. A power dialer doesn't make them from the way it dials.

Power dialer vs predictive dialer at a glance

They differ in how many calls go out per person and who decides that number. Both work through your list for you, so nobody types a number.

A power dialer places one call per rep, and when that call ends it rings the next lead on the list. A predictive dialer places several calls per agent, and software picks how many. It watches how often calls get answered, how long agents spend on each and how many agents are online, then times new calls so that someone answers just as an agent comes free. A good guess takes an agent from one conversation straight into the next. A bad one leaves a person on the line with nobody to talk to.

Why a predictive dialer needs a crowd

A predictive dialer bets that someone will finish a call just as someone else answers. Say 50 agents are on the floor: one of them is almost always about to hang up, so the bet pays. With three reps it often doesn't, and each lost bet is a lead who picked up to silence or a recording.

The handover is fast, too. The agent hears a connection and starts talking, often with a moment or less to see who's there. That suits scripted, high-volume work such as collections or surveys. In sales we'd want to know who we're calling before they say hello.

How a power dialer keeps the rep on the line

A power dialer calls from the rep's own line, one lead at a time, so the rep hears it ring and is there when the lead answers. The lead's contact page opens as they connect, and you can see who they are before you've finished your first sentence.

When the call ends, a short countdown runs before the next lead rings. The rep can skip it, add time or fill in notes while it waits. On a voicemail box, one click leaves a recorded message and the dialer moves on.

The trade-off is speed. A power dialer never rings more people than you can talk to, so on a cold list where most calls ring out, the rep waits through every unanswered ring.

Which one is easier to keep compliant?

The power dialer, by a wide margin. The FTC's own guide says "abandoned calls often result from telemarketers' use of predictive dialers to call consumers" (FTC, Complying with the Telemarketing Sales Rule, checked 2026). These are the rules a predictive dialer has to stay inside:

  • The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule, a US law, counts a call as abandoned when the person who answers isn't connected to a sales rep within two seconds of their greeting. Its safe harbor allows at most 3 percent of answered calls per campaign, measured over each 30-day period, and expects a recorded message with the seller's name and phone number when nobody is free (16 CFR 310.4, eCFR 2026).
  • The FCC's rule, also a US law, sets the same 3 percent limit on abandoned telemarketing calls over 30 days, and the seller must keep records showing it stays under it (47 CFR 64.1200, eCFR 2026).
  • Ofcom's policy on persistent misuse (2016) is a UK regulator's policy, setting out how Ofcom uses its powers under the Communications Act 2003. It covers silent and abandoned calls, expects an information message within two seconds when no agent is free, and treats silent calls as the more harmful kind.

A predictive dialer has to be tuned and measured, campaign by campaign, to stay inside those limits. Consent, do-not-call registers and calling hours apply to both, and under the CallForges Terms they're your responsibility. The rules depend on who you call and where.

Which dialer should your team use?

For most sales teams, the power dialer. We'd pick it when:

  • your team is small, from one founder up to a few dozen reps;
  • you call warm leads, trial users or existing customers, who answer often;
  • you call consumers in the US or the UK;
  • your reps need a few seconds to see who they're calling before the lead says hello.

A predictive dialer makes sense for a large call center with many agents on one script, calling big lists where few people answer, with someone whose job is to watch the abandonment rate.

Small teams with big cold lists have a middle option. A parallel dialer rings a few leads at once for a single rep and connects the first one who answers. You choose how many lines ring, so you decide how much risk to take, and it fits business-to-business lists where most calls go unanswered. Power dialer vs parallel dialer covers when to use each.
