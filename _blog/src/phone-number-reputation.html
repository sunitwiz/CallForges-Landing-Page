Phone number reputation is the score the called person's network and call-blocking app give your number, and a low score turns your calls into "Scam Likely" or "Potential Spam". Bursts of calls, short or unanswered calls and spam reports lower it; registering your numbers, disputing wrong labels and calling at a human pace protect it.

Key takeaways
  • Your own phone company does not set the label. The network of the person you call, or the app on their phone, does.
  • Volume alone can trip it: bursts of calls, many short or unanswered calls, and reports from people who did not want the call.
  • A free registry run by the analytics companies the big US networks use takes your business details and numbers. It makes a label less likely but does not guarantee one goes away.

What is phone number reputation?

Phone number reputation is a rating of your number, worked out by the network of the person you call or by a call-blocking app on their phone. When the rating is bad, their screen shows a warning instead of a plain call, or the call goes straight to voicemail.

The rating does not come from your phone company, and nobody fills it in by hand. The FCC describes these warning labels as "generally based on analytics" (FCC Seventh Report and Order, 2023). AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon each work with an analytics company on these labels (USTelecom, checked 2026).

That matters for a sales team, because a rating is judged from patterns, not intent. A team that calls a lot looks, to a machine, a little like a robocaller. Credit unions told the FCC that providers said analytics marked their calls as potential spam "based solely on the quantity of outbound calls" (FCC Second Report on Call Blocking, 2021).

What do spam labels look like?

People often call all of them "Spam Likely", but each network uses its own words. These are the warnings the FCC and Verizon list:

A label is not always the end of it. T-Mobile's Scam Block can block calls marked Scam Likely at the network, and Verizon's Call Filter can send spam calls straight to voicemail (FCC, 2021). So a bad rating can cost you the call, not only the pick-up.

What lowers a number's reputation?

Four things pull a rating down: many calls in a burst, very short or unanswered calls, complaints and spam reports, and a number that someone used badly before. A busy sales number can trip the first two without doing anything wrong.

  • Many calls in a burst. Verizon defines Potential Spam as "unwanted callers that may be calling indiscriminately or inappropriately to a large number of recipients" (Verizon Call Filter FAQs, checked 2026). The FCC tells phone companies to watch "large bursts of calls in a short time frame" (FCC, 2023).
  • Very short or unanswered calls. The same FCC list starts with "call duration" and "call completion ratios" (FCC, 2023). A list of numbers where most calls ring briefly and end unanswered looks like a robocall run.
  • Complaints and spam reports. Verizon Call Filter users can report numbers as spam, and the Phone app on Android lets users report suspicious calls (FCC, 2021). Every person who taps "report" after your call adds to the rating.
  • A number used badly before. Labels are tied to the number (FCC, 2021), and numbers get reused: about 35 million US numbers are disconnected and made available for reassignment each year (FCC Second Report and Order, 2018). A number can reach you with a rating it earned for someone else.

Signing your calls helps but does not replace a good rating. Verizon says it does not "block or label a call made to a Verizon customer based solely on caller ID authentication information" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026). How the number calls still counts.

How do you register your numbers with the analytics services?

Use the Free Caller Registry: one free form, run by the three call-analytics companies, that shares your numbers with all of them. Those three are the analytics partners of AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon (USTelecom, checked 2026).

The Free Caller Registry asks for (checked 2026):

  • Your business name, street address, city, state and ZIP code, and your website.
  • A contact name, phone number and a verified email address.
  • Why you call and how many outbound calls you make a month.
  • Your numbers: up to 20 typed in one by one, or a file.

Be clear about what it does. The registry says each company "may" use the data to improve its call management, and that registering "does not guarantee redress". It also says its data is not used to show your caller ID name, which is a separate registration (same source). Registering makes a label less likely. It does not remove one.

How do you check or dispute a spam label?

Check by calling phones on each big network from the number and looking at the screen. Dispute a wrong label with the form of the network that shows it. USTelecom, the phone companies' trade group, keeps one list of those forms (USTelecom, checked 2026):

Before you file, the industry's own guidance says to look at your calling habits first (USTelecom Best Practices Relating to Redress Requests, 2026). If a label still looks wrong, the same guidance says:

  • A standard request should be answered within 2 business days, and providers aim to meet that for at least 95% of requests. These are "best practices and not guarantees".
  • You should not be asked to pay a fee or sign up for anything else.
  • You do not need to register your numbers first.

Verizon's form takes up to 50 numbers, or a file for more, and warns that a submission "may not result in a change to how future calls are labeled" (Verizon spam feedback form, checked 2026). Send each number once and give a clear reason, such as "my business makes calls to people who asked us to call".

This is general information, not legal advice.

What calling habits protect a number?

Call like a person, not like a machine: spread calls over more numbers, pace them, call when people are around, and stop calling anyone who says no. These habits answer the signals above directly.

  1. Give each rep or team its own number. The same daily calls spread over several numbers make smaller bursts on each one.
  2. Pace the calls. Leave a pause between calls, and if you ring several leads at once, keep the number of lines modest. Fewer calls a minute means fewer bursts.
  3. Let the phone ring. Hanging up after two rings makes the short, unanswered calls the analytics look for.
  4. Call in the lead's working hours. A call at a sensible hour is more likely to be answered, and less likely to annoy someone into reporting it.
  5. Honor every "don't call me". Keep a do-not-call list and use it on every list you dial. Reports from annoyed people pull the rating down.
  6. Register and test every new number. Add it to your registrations before its first busy day, and call a phone on each network to see what shows.
  7. Don't swap numbers to dodge a label. A fresh number called the same way gets judged by the same analytics. Fix the habit, then dispute the label.

FAQ

Does registering remove a spam label?

No. Registering tells the analytics who you are and why you call, so a label is less likely. To clear a label that is already there, dispute it with the network that shows it.

Will a new number fix a spam label?

Only for a while, if nothing else changes. The new number is rated by the same analytics, from the same calling pattern. A reused number can also arrive with a bad rating of its own.
