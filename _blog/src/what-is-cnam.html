A lead who filled in your form an hour ago sees your callback come in as a bare number from a city they don't know. CNAM, short for caller name, is the business name that shows next to that number, such as ACME INC, and your call doesn't carry it: the called person's carrier looks your number up in a CNAM database and shows the name it finds. To show yours, you register it so it's in those databases.

Key takeaways
  • Caller ID is your number and CNAM is your name. Only the number travels with the call; the called person's carrier looks the name up.
  • No single registry holds every name. Several CNAM databases do, and each network reads its own or pays for the lookup.
  • Landlines show the name, many mobiles only with a caller ID service on, and a saved contact or a spam label replaces it.

Is CNAM the same as caller ID?

No. Caller ID is your number, and it travels with every call; CNAM is the name, which the called side adds by looking your number up. In the FCC's words, the phone network's signaling does "not transmit the name of the calling party along with the number", so caller ID services find the name "by sending a query to a centralized calling name (CNAM) database" (FCC Truth in Caller ID order, 2011).

What is the calling party name in CNAM?

It's the name a CNAM database holds for the number that's calling: for a business line, your business name, cut down to fit. The FCC describes CNAM databases as linking numbers "to the individuals and entities to whom the numbers have been assigned" (FCC report to Congress on caller ID, 2011).

Who keeps the name? CNAM databases and providers

No single CNAM registry holds every name. Several CNAM databases do, and on each call the called person's carrier runs a lookup, which the trade calls a dip: it sends your number to a database and gets back a name, or nothing.

The FCC counted "multiple CNAM databases used by providers of caller ID services" back in 2011 (FCC, 2011). Some carriers keep their own, and others buy the lookup from companies that gather listings "from a variety of sources", which can involve "a chain of feeds through several layers of providers and resellers" (FCC report to Congress, 2011). That gives two kinds of CNAM provider: a carrier with a database of its own, and a company that answers lookups for carriers without one.

As the caller, you don't pick either kind: the carrier behind your number files your name, and the feeds pass it along. The called person's carrier still chooses whether to show a name at all.

Why isn't my caller ID name showing?

Usually the phone you called doesn't show names, or something on it outranks yours. Caller name lookups began on landline phones (FCC, 2025), and on many mobile networks an unknown caller's name is a feature the person turns on or pays for, a carrier policy each network sets for itself.

A wrong or old name has another cause. The FCC noted in 2025 that CNAM databases "reportedly are not reliably accurate" (FCC, 2025), and a recycled number can keep a previous owner's listing on one network after another shows yours.

CNAM, STIR/SHAKEN and spam labels

STIR/SHAKEN does a different job from CNAM: your carrier signs the call so the called network can check the number wasn't faked, and the signature puts no name on the screen. The FCC says "legacy CNAM databases currently remain the only widespread source of caller name information", and in October 2025 it proposed requiring carriers to pass on a verified caller name with calls whose number is unlikely to be spoofed (FCC, 2025). In May 2026 that was still a proposal, not a rule (FCC, 2026). Our STIR/SHAKEN guide covers the signing levels.

A registered name won't stop a spam label either, because each carrier makes that call for itself. On AT&T's business phone service, a call judged to be spam is "delivered with Suspected Spam as the caller ID name" (AT&T Call Protect, checked 2026). Questions about CNAM

What does CNAM stand for?

Caller name, also called calling name: the FCC's 2011 report notes it "is also referred to as 'Calling' Name" (FCC, 2011). Its 2025 proposal writes "Caller ID Name (CNAM)".

What is CNAM delivery?

It's the called side's half of CNAM, a service on the called person's line: their carrier looks the name up "from a Caller Name (CNAM) database" and sends it to their phone (FCC, 2011). Their carrier runs it, so you can register a name but can't make a network show it.

How long does it take for CNAM to show?

In CallForges, 48 to 72 hours after the carriers approve the name, and the Business profile before it takes about 1 to 2 days. Each network reads its own database or buys the lookup (FCC, 2011), so your name can show on one network before another.

What is a CNAM API?

It lets your own software run a dip: send a number, get back the name on file. It reads names and can't set yours, which takes a registration for the number you call from.

Is CNAM only used in the US?
