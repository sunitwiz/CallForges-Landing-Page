A rep calls back a lead who filled in your form an hour ago, and the lead's phone shows a number from a city they don't know. CNAM (caller ID name) puts a short business name next to that number, such as ACME INC. Your call carries only the number: the called person's carrier looks the name up in a caller name database. To show your name, you register it so it's in those databases.

Key takeaways
  • Your name isn't sent with the call. The called person's carrier looks your number up in a caller name database and shows what it finds.
  • Landlines with caller ID show the name. Many mobiles show it only with a caller ID service turned on, and a saved contact shows the name it was saved under.
  • A spam label can replace your name, so a caller ID name works alongside STIR/SHAKEN and number reputation.

How your name reaches the phone you call

The called person's carrier looks the name up at the far end of the call: it takes your number, asks a caller name database which name belongs to it, and sends the answer to the phone. Without a name on file, the person sees your number, and usually its city and state.

In the FCC's words, the phone network's signaling does "not transmit the name of the calling party along with the number", so caller ID services find the name "by sending a query to a centralized calling name (CNAM) database" (FCC Truth in Caller ID order, 2011). No single database holds every name. Some carriers run their own, and others buy the lookup from companies that gather listings from many sources, which may involve "a chain of feeds through several layers of providers and resellers" (FCC report to Congress on caller ID, 2011).

Registering a name means getting it into those databases, which your carrier does for you. Because the called person's carrier does the lookup, it also decides whether to show a name at all, so what people see varies by carrier.

Why does my caller ID name show on some phones and not others?

Landlines with caller ID show it, and so do mobiles whose carrier shows caller names on that line. Caller name lookups began on landline phones (FCC, 2025). On some mobile networks, seeing an unknown caller's name is a feature the person has to turn on, or pay for. That's a carrier policy, and each network sets its own.

Verizon's help page is a clear example. It says a caller's name appears on a Verizon mobile only when all of these are true, and that on other networks "it's the person's service provider (not Verizon) that displays a caller's name" (Verizon Share Name ID FAQs, checked 2026):

  • The phone works with Verizon's Call Filter app.
  • The phone has Call Filter Plus or another caller ID service turned on.
  • The person hasn't saved your number as a contact.

On Verizon, caller ID comes with Call Filter Plus, at $3.99 a month (Verizon Call Filter FAQs, checked 2026). If a rep says the name didn't show on their own mobile, nothing is broken. Don't burn an afternoon testing on your team's mobiles. Call a landline, or a phone with caller ID turned on.

Fitting your name into 15 characters

Plan for 15 characters, spaces included. Verizon caps the names of its own customers at "up to 15 characters with at least 1 letter" (Verizon Share Name ID FAQs, checked 2026), and each provider sets its own allowed characters on top of that, so the details vary by carrier.

Fifteen characters go fast, and CallForges allows only letters, numbers, spaces, periods and commas:

How long does it take for CNAM to show?

A new name doesn't appear everywhere at the same moment. Each network reads its own database, or buys the lookup from someone else (FCC, 2011), so a name can show on one network before another. In CallForges, plan for two waits: about 1 to 2 days for your Business profile, then 48 to 72 hours after the carriers approve the name. If it shows on a landline but not on a mobile, check the mobile's caller ID setting before anything else.

CNAM, STIR/SHAKEN and spam labels

A caller ID name shows who's calling. STIR/SHAKEN proves the number is yours, and number reputation affects whether the person sees a spam warning instead of your name.

What it doesWhat the person sees
CNAM (caller ID name)Puts your business name on your number in the caller name databasesACME INC instead of a bare number
STIR/SHAKENYour carrier signs the call, so the called network can check the number wasn't fakedUsually nothing directly. Their network uses the signature when it judges the call.
Number reputationRegisters your numbers and why you call with the networks' spam analyticsA lower risk of a spam warning in place of your name

STIR/SHAKEN is a set of standards that lets "authenticated information about a call" travel with it, and its level A says the signing carrier knows its customer and that customer's right to use the number (FCC, 2025). It doesn't put your name on the screen. The FCC says "legacy CNAM databases currently remain the only widespread source of caller name information", and in October 2025 it proposed requiring carriers to pass on a verified caller name with calls whose number is unlikely to be spoofed (same source). That's a proposal, not a rule.

A registered name won't protect you from a spam label, which is each carrier's own call. On AT&T's business phone service, a call judged to be spam is "delivered with Suspected Spam as the caller ID name" (AT&T Call Protect, checked 2026). If your team makes a lot of calls, we'd register all three.
